Collaborative Comparative Lion Research

A jointly led programme across contrasting African lion populations.

We investigate how ecological conditions shape lion social behaviour, collective action, communication, and perception by comparing populations that experience very different ecological and social environments.

Funding & programme development

One programme built through three linked awards.

Each award expanded the same collaborative research programme: first establishing a comparative framework for sociality, then adding whole-group approaches to collective action, and finally extending the programme toward the sensory information available before animals act.

01 · 2021

Collaborative Research Grant · MPI-AB / University of Konstanz

When does lion sociality emerge—and when does it break down?

The starting problem: lions are social across environments that differ dramatically in resource abundance, density, and the opportunities individuals have to encounter one another.

This project established the comparative foundation of the programme by asking how social structure and cooperation vary across lion populations living under different ecological conditions. The goal is not simply to catalogue population differences, but to identify the conditions under which different social strategies become more or less advantageous.

What it enabled

A multi-population comparative framework linking ecological variation to social structure, cooperation, and movement.

That comparative framework raised a second question: if ecological conditions alter sociality, how are collective actions actually organized among individuals?
02 · 2022

CASCB Newcomer Project · Centre for the Advanced Study of Collective Behaviour

Cooperation in collective action: on the “hunt” for role specialisation.

The next problem: understanding why animals are social does not explain how multiple individuals coordinate a joint action.

This project moves from the ecology of sociality to the mechanics of collective action. By collecting fine-scale data simultaneously from entire prides, we ask how individuals communicate and respond to one another, whether they coordinate toward joint outcomes, and under what conditions specialized roles emerge during cooperative hunting.

What it added

Whole-group multi-sensor biologging, fine-scale measurement of collective movement, and explicit tests of communication, cooperative hunting, and role differentiation.

Award

€305,978

Official CASCB project page →
Measuring collective behaviour tells us what animals do together. It does not tell us what information was available to them before they moved, communicated, or coordinated.
03 · 2025

ZENiT Fellowship · Zukunftskolleg, University of Konstanz

What do animals perceive?

The mechanistic extension: what sensory information is actually available to lions when they make social decisions?

“What do animals perceive? Advancing Multi-Sensor Biologging for Sensory Ecology Research” extends the programme from observed collective behaviour to the perceptual environment that precedes it. We combine AI-supported analysis of multi-sensor biologging data with controlled experiments on how lions perceive sounds, sights, and smells—particularly in the contexts of communication and coordination.

What it adds

A sensory-ecology layer linking environmental information, perception, communication, and collective decision-making.

What the awards have built

A comparative platform for studying lion societies.

Together, the three awards support a shared comparative framework across ecologically contrasting lion populations. Whole-group biologging provides comparable measurements across populations, while field methods are adapted to the ecological and logistical realities of each system.

Across populations

Whole-group biologging

Simultaneous measurements from multiple group members allow social behaviour to be studied as interactions among individuals rather than as isolated movement tracks.

Across scales

Behaviour in ecological context

Movement, association, communication, resource conditions, and social opportunity are interpreted together rather than as separate datasets.

Across methods

Sensors grounded by field knowledge

Direct observation, tracking, and site-specific ecological knowledge provide the behavioural ground truth needed to interpret sensor data.

Comparative field systems

Different ecological conditions create different social opportunities.

The strength of the programme comes from applying a common comparative framework to lion populations that differ in resource availability, landscape structure, population density, and proximity to people.

Namibia

Mountains, scarcity, and shared space

In north-western Namibia, low-density lions move through a semi-arid, mountainous landscape used extensively by people and livestock. Through the co-led Ombonde Research Team, we combine whole-group biologging with long-term field monitoring across a landscape where human activity is not a distant boundary but part of the environment lions move through.

Programme role: Tests how social organisation operates when resources are sparse, groupmates may be widely dispersed, and lions share the entire landscape with people and livestock.

Low density Semi-arid Mountainous Shared landscape

Botswana

Pans, tracking, and the human edge

In Botswana, low-density lions occupy a predominantly flat, semi-arid landscape structured by pans and bordered by areas used by people and livestock. Our long-term collaboration with Leopard Ecology & Conservation combines whole-group biologging with San tracking expertise to reconstruct hunts, movements, and social interactions that sensor data alone cannot reveal.

Programme role: Links comparable biologging measurements to unusually detailed behavioural reconstruction on the ground.

Low density Semi-arid Flat / pans Human-use boundary San tracking

Zimbabwe

Resources, density, and social opportunity

Our Zimbabwe population provides a strong ecological contrast: resources are comparatively abundant, lion density is high, and the study lions are spatially removed from the immediate human-use boundary.

Programme role: Provides the high-resource, high-density comparison needed to ask how movement, association, and collective behaviour change when groupmates and resources are encountered more frequently.

High density Resource-rich High social opportunity Lower immediate human exposure

How the programme works

Comparable measurements, grounded in field knowledge.

No single technology reveals social behaviour on its own. We combine measurements across scales so that movement, interaction, communication, perception, and ecological context can be interpreted together.

Accelerometry & magnetometry
Collar-mounted acoustics
Direct behavioural observation
Indigenous tracking
Behavioural & sensory experiments
Comparative field ecology
AI-supported multi-sensor analysis
